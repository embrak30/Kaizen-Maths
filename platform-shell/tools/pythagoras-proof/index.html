A proof by rearrangement

Start with a right-angled triangle. Build an actual square outwards on each leg and mark the square on its hypotenuse. The two leg lengths can be changed independently; the right angle stays fixed.

This is Perigal’s dissection. Through the centre of the larger leg square, draw two perpendicular cuts parallel to the sides of the hypotenuse square. Slide these four pieces to the four corners of that square. The smaller leg square slides, without rotation, into the remaining central gap.

Each coloured piece keeps exactly the same shape and area. At the end there are no gaps and no overlaps. No extra pieces are added. Therefore the combined area a² + b² is exactly the hypotenuse square’s area c². The pieces may pass over one another while moving; only the final arrangement is a tiling.

The fit is general: in coordinates aligned with the hypotenuse square, the two cut edges meeting at the centre of the larger square each have length c/2. These become half of each outer side. The remaining central gap is a square, parallel to the original leg squares, whose side is the shorter leg. Equal legs also work: the four pieces become triangles.

The triangle is always drawn to scale. If b is longer than a, the diagram places b horizontally so the same dissection can be used. The colour key follows the labels. Triples give whole-number examples; decimals and equal legs show that the argument is not restricted to triples. Rounded lengths are marked ≈.
