Area by Rearrangement

From paper shapes to area formulae

Use the four stages to introduce the shape, prepare the pieces, rearrange them, and connect the area. The movement slider lets you pause or reverse the demonstration. Change the shape or sector count without leaving the workspace. Animation never changes a piece’s size.

Circle: a limiting argument

Cut a paper circle into equal, even-numbered sectors. Colour one half teal and the other gold. Alternate the sectors, with the teal arcs below and the gold arcs above. Each curved boundary contains half the circumference, πr, but its straight span is shorter.

For n sectors, each sector angle is 2πn. The straight span between the marked endpoints is nr sin(πn), and the marked height is r cos(πn). The small curved bumps extend above and below that height. As n increases without bound, the span approaches πr, the height approaches r, and the bumps shrink to zero. The rearrangement approaches a rectangle of area πr². Every finite sector arrangement is still curved; the dashed rectangle is the limiting comparison, not a claim that a finite arrangement is rectangular.

The original circle and its rearrangement have exactly the same area at every sector count. The motion may overlap pieces temporarily; use the completed arrangement for the area comparison. This is a visual limiting argument, rather than a substitute for a formal treatment of limits.

Parallelogram: cut and translate

Draw a perpendicular from the top-left vertex to the base. Cut off the teal triangle and slide it horizontally by one base length. It fills the gap at the right, forming a rectangle. Nothing is stretched or discarded. The height is the perpendicular distance between the parallel bases, not the sloping side. Try both slants.

Triangle: duplicate and rotate

Make a second identical triangle. Rotate it through 180° about the midpoint of the right-hand side. The two triangles form a parallelogram with the same base and perpendicular height as the original triangle. Its area is bh, so each triangle has half that area. Try acute, right-angled and obtuse examples. The obtuse example places the perpendicular height outside the original triangle.

Recreate in class

Use paper, scissors, two colours, a ruler and tracing paper for the duplicate triangle. Pause before the final stage and ask pupils to predict the new shape, identify its base and height, and explain why the area is preserved.
